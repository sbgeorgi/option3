What you are replacing: what Lonely Planet sells

Wikipedia describes Lonely Planet as a travel guidebook publisher founded in Australia in 1973, which has printed over 150 million books and is owned by Red Ventures. Its shop sells "official guidebooks, travel maps, gift books, kids books" in print and digital, and its destination pages open without signing in. Its app page promises expert guides, itineraries, walking tours and must-do experiences on iOS and Android.

So "alternatives to Lonely Planet books" really means three jobs: a printed or ebook guide to read, a web guide, and a tool to turn what you read into a plan. The options below cover those in that order.

Lonely Planet alternatives, one by one

Rough Guides: the like-for-like guidebook swap

Its shop page says travel guides are "available in paperback and ebook format", that Rough Guides has been "inspiring travellers for over 35 years" and that it has sold "over 30 million copies globally". The shop lets you browse by region and by type (itineraries, pocket-sized, inspirational, maps). Its homepage now leads with tailor-made trips planned with local experts, a travel-agency service.

  • Fits: you want a book or ebook with a similar role to a Lonely Planet country guide.
  • Does not fit: the pages I read give no revision schedule, so check each title's edition date before you buy.

Different need: NomadSwipe, to pick the places rather than read about them

I built NomadSwipe (Android). It is not a guidebook. You pick a city, see real places one card at a time, tap Save or swipe right to keep one and Skip or swipe left to pass. A card shows the category, rating, distance and opening status, saved places go on a map for the trip, and the list sorts by rating, distance or A to Z. It works in any city worldwide, and you can rank places as food, sights, bars or stays.

  • Fits: you already have a destination and want a shortlist of places you chose yourself, on a map, before planning days.
  • Does not fit: it has no written guide text, history or practical tips, which is what a Lonely Planet book is for. Use both together.

Rick Steves: Europe only, with a visible update page

The Guidebook Updates page says "Our books are updated frequently and thoroughly" and that it posts "all the critical changes" found since the books went to print, country by country. The countries listed run from Austria to Wales, so it is a Europe specialist. The homepage also sells small-group tours.

  • Fits: a Europe trip where you want a guide that publishes its own corrections.
  • Does not fit: anywhere outside the countries it lists.

DK travel guides: picture-led and pocket-sized

The DK travel page says its "hyper visual guides" use "simple formats and layouts" to help plan a trip, and it lists DK Top 10 pocket guides for individual cities. The format filter includes paperback and EBook. The page does not say how often titles are revised.

  • Fits: a short city break where a small, visual book is enough.
  • Does not fit: a deep, text-heavy country read; the page I read lists mostly pocket city titles.

Wikivoyage: the web guide anyone can edit

Its About page calls it "a free, complete, up-to-date, and reliable worldwide travel guide" hosted by the Wikimedia Foundation and written by volunteers. It says it is "continually edited and updated", unlike guides revised "monthly or annually", and it even answers the question "when there are already well-developed travel guides such as Lonely Planet?". Those are its own claims; any volunteer can edit, so coverage differs by destination.

  • Fits: reading up on a destination before you decide whether to buy a book.
  • Does not fit: a printed copy to carry.

GPSmyCity: ready-made walks

The GPSmyCity homepage describes "GPS-powered self-guided walking tours and travel tips for 1,800+ cities", over 7,000 walks, turn-by-turn directions and offline use, plus a way to create your own walk in 670+ cities. More in GPSmyCity alternatives; what costs money is in GPSmyCity free or paid.

  • Fits: following a walk someone else designed.
  • Does not fit: choosing your own places across many cities; the page gives no price or update schedule, so I give none.

Wanderlog: plan the trip around what you read

Wanderlog's homepage says you can "create detailed itineraries, explore user-shared guides, and manage your bookings" in one app, with the itinerary and map in one view. See how to use Wanderlog or other apps like Wanderlog.

  • Fits: turning guide recommendations into a day-by-day plan.
  • Does not fit: authoritative text; user-shared guides are only as good as their authors.

Google Maps lists: the zero-setup notebook

Google's help page says "you can create a list of places, like your favorite places or places you want to visit", private or shared by link. Another Google page says "Saved Lists are limited to 3,000 entries". More in Google Maps lists compared.

  • Fits: collecting every place a book recommends, in an app already on your phone.
  • Does not fit: it holds places but writes nothing about them.

Lonely Planet app alternative: which apps do the app's job

Lonely Planet's app page lists guides, itineraries and walking tours. The nearest app matches are GPSmyCity for walks and Wanderlog for itineraries. Similar to Lonely Planet: how to choose

  • A book or ebook to read: Rough Guides, Rick Steves (Europe) or DK.
  • Reading online: Wikivoyage.
  • A walk to follow: GPSmyCity.
  • A plan from places you picked: NomadSwipe (Android), Wanderlog or a Google Maps list. The method is in how to find things to do in a new city.

Bottom line

For another printed or ebook guide, start with Rough Guides, or Rick Steves if you are going to Europe. To read online, use Wikivoyage. To turn what you read into places on a map on Android, use NomadSwipe (I built it), Wanderlog or a Google Maps list. Pages and prices change, so check each source before you rely on it.

FAQ

What is the closest alternative to Lonely Planet books?

Another guidebook publisher. Rough Guides sells travel guides in paperback and ebook, and its shop page says it has sold over 30 million copies worldwide. Rick Steves covers Europe only, and DK sells visual and pocket guides. Which one fits depends on where you are going, so check your destination on each shop.

Is there a free alternative to Lonely Planet?

Wikivoyage describes itself as a free, worldwide travel guide written by volunteers, with text under a Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike license. It is edited continuously rather than printed, so quality varies by destination. A Google Maps list can then hold the places you pick.

Does Lonely Planet have an app, and what are the alternatives?

Yes. Its app page says the Lonely Planet app has expert guides, itineraries, walking tours and must-do experiences, on iOS and Android. Alternatives that do part of that job are GPSmyCity (self-guided walking tours), Wanderlog (a trip planner with user-shared guides) and NomadSwipe (swipe real places and save them, Android only).
