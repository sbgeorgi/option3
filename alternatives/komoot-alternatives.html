Key facts

  • The Komoot home page describes route planning for road bikes, mountain bikes and hiking, with turn-by-turn voice navigation and offline maps. Its support article komoot plans: Maps and Premium says the money side works like this: Maps are one-time purchases (a single region, a region bundle or a World Pack) and Premium is a subscription. You can plan routes anywhere, but to navigate or record, your region must be activated, and first-time users get one single region free.
  • Per that article, offline functionality, voice navigation and GPX download are in both plans. Premium adds worldwide maps and navigation, multi-day routes, weather on route, live tracking, 3D maps and local topographic maps such as OS maps. Komoot's marketing pages blur this split, and I list the differences below. For its offline maps, see how to use Komoot offline.

What Komoot actually is

Komoot is an outdoor route planner and navigator. The home page leads with planning "sport-specific routes": smooth asphalt for a road bike, singletracks for a mountain bike, peaceful trails for hiking. It lets you filter routes by distance, difficulty or public transport links, navigate with turn-by-turn voice guidance and offline maps, and share photos and tips with its community. The Play listing is titled "komoot - hike, bike & run" and adds a GPS tracker for recording activities and "Highlights", places that other users recommend, shown as red dots in the planner, from peaks and parks to singletracks and sandwich shops.

The features page lists what the planner shows: heatmaps of popular paths, a ground-level trail view, elevation profiles, custom waypoints, a surface type breakdown and difficulty details. Navigation adds automatic re-routing if you leave the route. It names iOS and Android apps, Garmin and Wahoo integration and a standalone Apple Watch app. On the product updates page, October 2026 shows eight updates, among them a redesigned Wear OS app that navigates and records on the watch with everything working offline, and wildfire warnings that combine NASA satellite data with official fire services in Europe, the US and Canada.

Komoot's own numbers, as printed on its features page: a 4.7 average store rating, 40M+ downloads, 150+ countries, 8M+ routes and 850M+ photos and highlights. Google Play showed 4.5 from 443K reviews and 10M+ downloads on 15 November 2026, with the listing updated on 12 November. Those are two different measures, and I did not verify either beyond reading them.

Why people look for apps like Komoot

I did not survey anyone. The first two reasons have a source I read; the last three are my own inference from the comparison, and I say so.

  • Paying per region. Under the Maps plan, navigation only works in the regions you bought, and the worldwide option is the Premium subscription. One 2020 Play review on the listing says the regions are too small and expensive to justify the price. That is one old review, not a trend, but it names the same sore spot. For how another outdoor app splits free from paid, see AllTrails Plus vs free.
  • Missing trails. The same review says some trails in the reviewer's area were absent. Komoot's reply, dated June 2020 on the listing, says its planning is based on OpenStreetMap data and asks users to report missing ways. Its own Adventure Hub carries an article titled "Can I Trust the Komoot Map? A Guide to How Komoot Uses OSM". An OSM-based map is only as complete as OSM is in your area, which is a reason to test a second app on your home trails.
  • Official or specialist maps (my inference). Komoot lists local topographic maps such as OS maps as a Premium item. If national topographic maps are the main draw, Outdooractive and Gaia GPS put them at the centre of their pages.
  • One sport only (my inference). A road or gravel cyclist may prefer an app built just for cycling, which is Ride with GPS.
  • A different job (my inference). Some people searching "apps like Komoot" want somewhere to go, not a track to follow. That is where NomadSwipe fits, below.

Where the Komoot sources disagree

Reading five Komoot pages side by side, I found these mismatches. None means anyone is wrong, but each changes what you can safely say.

  1. Where offline maps sit. The features page banner offers a Premium week and says it includes offline maps, and the Premium page lists "Worldwide maps and navigation" as a Premium item. The support article lists offline functionality, voice navigation and GPX download in both Maps and Premium, with the difference that Maps cover only the regions you bought. The home page says a free account lets you "find, customize, and navigate" adventures, while the support article says navigating needs an activated region and gives first-time users one free single region. The marketing pages read as shorthand, so I take the structure from the support article. I did not make an account to test it.
  2. Review counts. The Play header showed 443K reviews and the ratings block lower on the same page showed 419K. I quote the header figure.
  3. Ratings and downloads. Komoot's pages print 4.7 and 40M+ downloads, Google Play showed 4.5 and 10M+. The 4.7 is described as an average store rating and the 40M+ is not tied to a store, so the two sets are not the same measure.
  4. Prices. None of the Komoot pages I read showed a price for Maps or Premium, and komoot.com/pricing and komoot.com/premium/pricing both returned 404 pages, so I give none. Check the current price in the app before you buy.

Alternatives to Komoot, one at a time

Outdooractive: official topo maps and Alpine club maps

Komoot lists local topographic maps such as OS maps as a Premium item. Outdooractive builds its pitch on that kind of map. Its Play listing counts official topo maps in 25 countries, from Ordnance Survey in the UK to USGS in the US, IGN in France and Swisstopo in Switzerland, plus the official Alpine Club maps for climbing in the Alps, and shows 4.5 from 78.2K reviews. The site adds a snow-depth layer, a Skyline augmented-reality view for naming peaks, BuddyBeacon live-location sharing (Komoot puts live tracking in Premium) and planning and printing for over 30 activities. It says Pro and Pro+ members get "the best maps" and extra features, but it lists the topo maps among its features without saying which tier includes them, and its Pro and subscription pages returned 404s. So I cannot tell you what is behind the paywall. Choose it over Komoot if official topo maps and Alpine routes matter most.

Ride with GPS: cycling only

The Ride with GPS site calls itself a planning and navigation app "just for cyclists", with over 3 million riders and downloads. Its pricing page splits the app in two: a free Starter plan with route exploring, ride recording in the mobile app, route planning on the website and device syncing, and Premium at $6.67 a month billed annually with a 7-day trial, which adds turn-by-turn voice navigation, offline maps, live logging, global heatmaps and printable custom maps. That makes it the clearest example here of navigation and offline maps sitting behind a paid plan. I could not load its Play listing, so I have no store rating for it. It does not cover hiking, which Komoot does.

Gaia GPS: map layers for the backcountry

The Gaia GPS site offers a free tier and a Premium plan with exclusive Gaia Hike and Gaia Overland maps, 300+ layers such as cell coverage and private land, and offline maps. It showed Premium at $3.24 a month billed annually ($38.93 for the first year, marked 35 percent off) after a 14-day trial, and an Outside+ bundle at $58.49 for the first year. The Play listing shows 3.4 from 22.4K reviews, the lowest rating in this comparison, and says Gaia is part of Outside, Inc. and that you create an Outside account to use the app. One listing review complains about that account step and about map downloads; I cannot say how common that is. It leans toward US public-land and backcountry use, and its pages say less about cycling than Komoot's.

Mapy.com: a hiking and cycling planner with country-sized offline maps

The Mapy.com Play listing describes an outdoor route planner with tracker and navigation, world maps in outdoor, aerial and winter styles, hiking, mountain bike and road bike modes, voice navigation in 32 languages and location sharing. It shows 4.1 from 260K reviews and says it contains ads. Premium is described as unlimited offline maps, advanced route planning and custom speed settings, so downloading more than one country needs a subscription. The listing does not say what the free tier allows. One September 2025 review on it describes the free tier as one whole country map and puts the US map at over 33 GB; I cannot confirm that. The Mapy.com site carries a 6 November 2026 note that downloaded offline maps now update automatically.

OsmAnd: a map toolbox instead of regional purchases

Where Komoot sells regions, OsmAnd sells map downloads in bulk. Its Play listing shows 4.5 from 255K reviews and says Maps+ (bought in-app or as a subscription) gives unlimited map downloads, contour lines and terrain data, nautical depths and offline Wikipedia and Wikivoyage guides, while OsmAnd Pro, a subscription, adds cloud backup, hourly map updates, a weather plugin and sensor support. The site says there are no ads even in the free version, and describes drawing your own route point by point and saving it as a GPX track. What the pages do not show is Komoot's route-discovery side: no community Highlights, no curated Collections. If you plan routes yourself and want one app for car, bike, boat and hike, test OsmAnd; if you want ready-made routes from other people, it is a weaker match. Where NomadSwipe fits (and where it does not)

For choosing where to go in a city, not how to walk or ride there

I built NomadSwipe, so weigh this with that in mind. It is an Android app for a different job than Komoot. Pick a city and it deals real restaurants, sights, bars and stays one card at a time. Swipe right or press Save to keep one. Your saved places are pinned on a travel map, and you can sort the list by rating, distance or A to Z. Cards show category, rating, distance and, where hours are known, whether the place is open. You can add custom pins for things the cards do not show, such as your hotel. Its Google Play listing, read on 15 November 2026, shows 50+ downloads and an example trip: a long weekend in New York with 16 places, all picked by swiping.

Where it is not the right tool: it does not plan a hike or a ride, record your track, show elevation or surface type, draw trails, or give turn-by-turn route guidance. The listing says you can open a place and start directions, but I did not test what that does. If your Komoot use is a mountain-bike loop or a hut-to-hut trek, NomadSwipe will not replace it. If your trip is a city break and the question is which twelve places are worth the time, it can sit next to Komoot: choose the places in NomadSwipe, then plan any walk or ride between them in a route app. I make no claim that a list moves between the two apps automatically. iPhone is coming soon.

FAQ

What are the best apps like Komoot?

From the pages I read on 15 November 2026: Outdooractive for official topo maps in many countries, Ride with GPS if you only cycle, Gaia GPS for backcountry map layers, Mapy.com for a hiking and cycling planner with country-sized offline maps, and OsmAnd for the most routing controls. I did not install any of them, so this is what each page says.

Is Komoot free?

Partly. Komoot's support article komoot plans: Maps and Premium says you can plan routes anywhere in the world, and first-time users get one free single region. To navigate or record elsewhere you activate a region with a one-time Maps purchase or subscribe to Premium. I read no price for either, so I quote none.

What is the difference between komoot Maps and Premium?

Per the support article, Maps are one-time purchases (a single region, a region bundle or the World Pack) limited to the regions you bought, while Premium is a subscription with worldwide maps and navigation. Premium also adds multi-day routes, weather on route, live tracking, 3D maps and local topographic maps such as OS maps. Offline functionality and voice navigation are in both.

Does Komoot work offline?

Yes. The support article lists offline functionality in both the Maps plan and Premium, with Maps limited to the regions you bought. The features page banner says Premium includes offline maps, so the marketing wording is looser than the support article. I did not test the app.

What is the best Komoot alternative for cycling only?

Ride with GPS is the one built only for cycling: its home page calls it a planning and navigation app just for cyclists. Its pricing page lists voice navigation and offline maps under Premium and route exploring, ride recording and device syncing under its free Starter plan. Komoot, Outdooractive and Mapy.com cover hiking as well.

Is there a Komoot alternative with official topo maps?

Komoot itself lists local topographic maps such as OS maps, but as a Premium item. Outdooractive names official topo maps from national mapping agencies: its Google Play listing counts 25 countries, including Ordnance Survey in the UK, USGS in the US, IGN in France and Swisstopo in Switzerland. Gaia GPS advertises 300+ map layers and says its USGS and USFS topos are among them.

Is NomadSwipe like Komoot?

No. I built NomadSwipe, an Android app where you swipe real places in a city, save the ones you like, see them on a map and build a trip. Komoot plans and navigates outdoor routes and records activities. NomadSwipe does none of that, so it is a different tool, not a replacement.

Can NomadSwipe record a hike or navigate a trail?

No. I built NomadSwipe, and its Google Play listing describes no GPS recording, no trail maps and no turn-by-turn route navigation. It says you can open a place and start directions; I did not test what that does. For trails, use one of the route apps compared on this page.

Is NomadSwipe on iPhone?

Not yet. NomadSwipe is on Google Play for Android phones, and iPhone is coming soon.
