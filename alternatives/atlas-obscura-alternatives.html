Apps like Atlas Obscura: 6 ways to find odd and hidden places

Updated 10 November 2026. Disclosure: I built NomadSwipe, an Android trip planner, and it is one of the six tools below. I did not install the other apps for this page. Every fact about them comes from a public page I read on 10 November 2026, linked where it is used.

Apps like Atlas Obscura are hard to find, because I found no direct twin for its community database of 33,790 places and foods. The nearest options each cover one part: Roadside America for US and Canada oddities, Geocaching for hidden containers, and Mapstr or Google Maps lists for keeping what you find.

What Atlas Obscura does, so you know what you are replacing

Atlas Obscura's about page calls it "a global community of explorers" that has built a database of 33,790 places and foods, "contributed by our community so far". Its Apple listing description lists four things: the whole database plotted on one map, a backstory for each entry, custom lists plus a "Been There" tracker, and browsing by interest such as history, architecture, art and natural wonders. Its homepage also shows a members tier with a monthly newsletter, recordings of virtual events and trip ideas, and it sells small-group trips.

That is the reason no single app replaces it: the value is a large set of curated odd places with stories, written by a community. The alternatives below either cover a narrower set of places or help you keep and plan the places you find elsewhere.

Atlas Obscura alternatives, one by one

Roadside America: offbeat attractions in the US and Canada

The Roadside America homepage describes itself as "Your Online Guide to Offbeat Tourist Attractions" with "30+ years of sightings and advice". Its state and province menu covers the US and Canada, plus a Mexico option and an "Other" category, and the page describes an app for iPhone and iPad with on-route maps, tips and photos. It does not state a total number of attractions or a price for the app, so I will not give either.

  • Strength: a road-trip lens on giant statues, museums of the odd and other roadside stops.
  • Limit: the menu is North America, so it is no help for Lisbon or Chiang Mai. The page describes an iPhone and iPad app and does not mention Android.

Geocaching: finding hidden containers outdoors

The Geocaching homepage calls it "the world's largest treasure hunt": you use the official app or a GPS device to find a geocache, sign the logbook inside, return it to where it was and log the find online. It states 3,490,089 geocaches around the world, and says the app is on iOS and Android.

  • Strength: an enormous number of hidden spots, often in places a guidebook skips, with a game on top.
  • Limit: the target is a container, not a sight with a story. Many caches are not "places" in the Atlas Obscura sense, and the homepage does not say what a typical cache looks like.

Mapstr: a map for the odd places you already found

Mapstr's homepage (in French) says you can save your addresses and organize them on an interactive map, add custom tags and comments, and import addresses from Google Maps and Foursquare. You can make a map private or public, follow friends' maps and create collaborative maps. It has iOS and Android listings and a web version. Its page showed no consumer price, so I give none. For a longer look, see Mapstr alternatives.

  • Strength: a tidy home for a personal list of places across many cities.
  • Limit: it holds places you add or import. The page describes no database of odd places to browse.

Google Maps lists: the zero-setup way to keep a pile of places

Google's help page says "In Google Maps, you can create a list of places, like your favorite places or places you want to visit." A list is Private ("Only you can view and edit") or Shared ("Share a link to view or edit"). A separate Google page says "Saved Lists are limited to 3,000 entries"; more on that limit is in this comparison. Google Maps does not carry Atlas Obscura's stories, so you paste in places you found elsewhere.

  • Strength: already on your phone, and a shared list is one link.
  • Limit: it is storage and a map. It will not tell you which places are odd.

Wanderlog: odd places from other travellers' guides

Wanderlog's homepage describes itinerary building and bookings in one place, with "user-shared guides" you can explore and a feature to "add places from guides with 1 click". Its Apple listing describes a travel app with an optional Pro subscription for planning trips and viewing places on a map. The site's own claims are marketing statements I did not verify. See how to use Wanderlog for more.

  • Strength: a planner that can pull places from guides other people wrote, so an odd-places guide can become part of a plan.
  • Limit: it is a planner first. Whether a guide covers odd places depends on who wrote it.

NomadSwipe: an honest fit for the trip, not the odd-places hunt

I built NomadSwipe, an Android trip planner. You pick a city and it shows real places one card at a time; tap Save or swipe right to keep one, tap Skip or swipe left to pass. A card shows the category, rating, distance and opening status, saved places are pinned on a map for that trip, and the list can be sorted by rating, distance or A to Z. Places are ranked as food, sights, bars or stays, so you can work through sights only.

  • Fits: you already have a city and want a quick shortlist of places you picked yourself, on a map, before planning days.
  • Does not fit: it is not an odd-places database. It has no stories, no community-written entries and no "weird" filter, so Atlas Obscura finds the strange spots better.
  • Does not fit: it is Android only, and iPhone is coming soon.

Bottom line

Atlas Obscura has no single replacement among the tools read for this page: its 33,790 community-written places and foods are the product. For odd places in the US and Canada use Roadside America, for hidden containers use Geocaching, to keep what you find use Mapstr or a Google Maps list, and to shortlist a city trip on Android use NomadSwipe (I built it). Prices and counts change, so check each page before you rely on a number.

FAQ

Is there an app exactly like Atlas Obscura?

Not among the tools read for this page. Atlas Obscura says its database holds 33,790 places and foods contributed by its community. The alternatives here cover part of the job: Roadside America for offbeat attractions in the US and Canada, Geocaching for hidden containers, and Mapstr or Google Maps lists for keeping the odd places you find.

Does Atlas Obscura have an app for Android and iPhone?

Its Apple listing shows an app called Atlas Obscura Travel Guide, with 4.85 from 9,930 ratings when read on 10 November 2026. Its website also shows a Get it on Google Play badge, so Android is covered too.

Is Roadside America an Atlas Obscura alternative?

For offbeat attractions in the US and Canada, yes. Its homepage calls it an online guide to offbeat tourist attractions, and its state and province menu covers the US and Canada, with Mexico and Other options. It is not a worldwide database.

Is NomadSwipe an Atlas Obscura alternative?

Only partly. I built NomadSwipe, an Android trip planner: you pick a city, swipe through real places, save the ones you like and see them on a map. It is not a database of odd places, so use Atlas Obscura to find the strange spots and NomadSwipe to shortlist a city trip.

How do I find hidden places in a city without Atlas Obscura?

Pick a boundary, filter to sights, and check each candidate by name and review count. The guide on this site, How to find hidden city gems, sets out one rule you can test and where it fails.
