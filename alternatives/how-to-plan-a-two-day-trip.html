How to plan a two day trip: six blocks, worked for Edinburgh

Here is how to plan a two day trip: pick one base, give each day its own neighborhood, and give each of the six blocks (morning, afternoon, evening) one anchor place. Book only what has a timed ticket or a sell-out warning, and add a 20 to 30 minute buffer after every block.

The method: seven steps

Two days give six blocks: morning, afternoon and evening, twice. On a travel day the first morning and the last afternoon are partly lost, so I treat them as the blocks I am willing to shorten. These steps turn a city and a list of ideas into those six blocks.

  1. Fix the frame.

    Write the bed address, when you arrive on day 1 and when you must leave on day 2. Those three facts decide how much of each day is real.

  2. Pick a base and a radius.

    In a walking city I measure every place from the bed in straight-line kilometers. Anything much past 1.5 km is a second trip, not a stop.

  3. List 8 to 12 candidates and write their hours first.

    Write opening time, last entry and closed days beside each name before you choose. A place without hours on the page is optional until you have checked it.

  4. Place the hardest constraint first.

    Find the earliest last entry, the timed ticket or the sell-out warning, and give that place the first block that fits. Everything else flexes around it.

  5. Split the days by neighborhood, not by category.

    A day is one connected stretch of the city. Add up the hops: about 4 km or less of walking is a day I would call comfortable.

  6. Give every block one anchor, up to two optional places and a buffer.

    The anchor is the block's one fixed point, the place the other choices bend around. Add a 20 to 30 minute buffer after each block for queues, a wrong turn or a coffee.

  7. Book the few, leave the rest open, and write one rain swap.

    Book what has a timed ticket or a sell-out warning. Spread your indoor places across both days, so wet weather means moving one block instead of rebuilding the plan.

Worked example: I planned two days in Edinburgh

I picked Edinburgh because its main sights publish their hours on official sites and sit within walking distance of each other. I ran the seven steps for a trip in November or December and wrote down what each step produced.

  1. Frame.

    I assumed a bed within walking distance of Edinburgh Waverley station, arrival before 9:30 on day 1 and a train out on the evening of day 2. Edinburgh Castle's own page calls Waverley the main and most central station and says the castle is a short walk away, "uphill all the way". The season matters, because the castle's hours change on 1 October and again after 23 December.

  2. Base and radius.

    My base is the bed by Waverley, so I measured every candidate from it. I took the coordinates Wikipedia lists for each place and computed straight-line distances (haversine). The farthest candidate, Holyrood Palace, is 1.05 km from Waverley, inside my 1.5 km radius. For walking time I used my own estimate, not a measured time: straight-line distance times 1.3 for street detours, at 4 km/h because of the hills. Treat each figure as roughly right to a tenth of a kilometer, since the coordinates mark a building, not its door.

  1. Hardest constraint first.

    The castle wins. Its last entry in this season is 4pm, the page suggests at least 2 hours, and it warns that tickets often sell out. An afternoon start would leave almost no margin, so it takes day 1 morning at the 9:30 opening, and it is the one thing I would book ahead.

  1. Split the days.

    Day 1 follows the Royal Mile spine, castle first and then downhill: about 3.5 km in total, counting the street at 1.6 km. Day 2 is a chain of short hops from Chambers Street to The Mound to Calton Hill: about 2.7 km. The castle and the National Museum are only 0.70 km apart, so they could share a day. I split them on purpose, so each day holds one indoor block I can swap when it rains.

  2. Fill the six blocks.

    One anchor per block, optional places only where the hours allow, and a hop and buffer on each row.

  1. Book, leave open, swap.

    I would book the castle only. The two museums list daily hours, so they stay open cells, and meals stay open too. The rain row is what those open cells are for. Optional places are the first to go: a queue at the castle costs the café and the gun, not the walk down the Royal Mile.

What the sources changed in my plan

  • The castle's hours fix the order. Last entry at 4pm in this season and a suggested 2 hours leave almost no margin for an afternoon start, so the castle goes first, at the 9:30 opening.
  • Closures are posted by date. The castle page I read on 24 November says the Scottish National War Memorial closes early, at 3pm, on Tuesday 24 November. A part-closure like that appears only on the page, so check it the day before.
  • Holyrood stays optional. I could not read its hours, so it cannot be an anchor until someone does.
  • The One o’Clock Gun depends on the weekday. It does not fire on Sundays, so a Sunday at the castle has no gun to plan around.

What to book and what to leave open

Rule of thumb applied to the Edinburgh plan
Book aheadLeave open
Anything with a timed ticket or an official sell-out warning (the castle)Meals, apart from one dinner you would hate to miss
Beds and the way out on day 2Museums with daily hours and no admission charge
Anything that closes on one weekdayWalks and viewpoints, which are your optional cells

The reason is flexibility. Every booked slot is a fixed point, and a plan with six fixed points has no room for a queue. Keep two or three blocks open and the plan absorbs the delay.

Questions

How many places should I plan for a two day trip?

Six anchors, one per block. The Edinburgh plan on this page has the castle, the Royal Mile, the National Museum and the Scottish National Gallery plus two dinners, and every other stop is optional. Cap the optional places at two per block, so a late start costs you a bonus and never an anchor.

How early should the first day start?

Start at the opening time of the place with the tightest hours, and put that place in the first block. In the Edinburgh plan that is the castle at 9:30, because its last entry is 4pm in November and its own page suggests at least 2 hours.

Should I book everything on a two day trip?

No. Book what has a timed ticket or an official warning that it sells out, and leave meals, walks and museums with daily hours open. Open cells are what let you swap a block when it rains or a queue eats an hour.

How far apart can places be on the same day?

I keep every place within about 1.5 km of the bed and a day under about 4 km of walking, before hills and detours. That is a rule of thumb of mine, not a standard. In the Edinburgh plan every place is within 1.05 km of Waverley, day 1 comes to about 3.5 km and day 2 to about 2.7 km.

The short version

  • A two day trip is six blocks: one anchor each, up to two optional places, and a 20 to 30 minute buffer.
  • Write the hours first, give the place with the tightest hours the first block, and split the days by neighborhood.
  • Book only what has a timed ticket or a sell-out warning, and write one rain swap by spreading indoor places across both days.
  • In my Edinburgh plan that meant the castle on the morning of day 1, the Royal Mile downhill after it, then the National Museum and the Scottish National Gallery on day 2.
