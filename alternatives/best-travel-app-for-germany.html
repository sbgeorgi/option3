Best Travel App for Germany: Which App for Which Job

Disclosure: I built NomadSwipe (Android). It is one row of six and I say where it does not fit. Every other fact comes from an official page or store listing I read on 15 November 2026. Published 15 November 2026. Updated 15 November 2026.

The best travel app for Germany depends on the job, and I installed none of these to crown one. For choosing what to see, I built NomadSwipe (Android), which works in any city worldwide and does only that. For trains and the Deutschland-Ticket, DB Navigator. For buses, trams and metro, the city's own app: BVG in Berlin, MVV in Munich, hvv in Hamburg.

Key facts

  • Germany splits into four jobs: rail between cities, transit inside each city, the nationwide Deutschland-Ticket, and deciding what to see. No app I read does all four.
  • NomadSwipe works in any city worldwide. It does not sell tickets, plan journeys or navigate.
  • DB's Deutschland-Ticket page says €63 per month, €66.80 from 1 January 2027, valid on local public transport and not in ICE or IC/EC trains. The European Consumer Centre Germany says it is sold only as a subscription.
  • The tourist board's getting-around text puts the rail network at about 40,000 kilometres with around 5,700 stations, run mainly by Deutsche Bahn but also by other operators.
  • Berlin, Munich and Hamburg each have their own operator app that sells tickets, according to BVG, MVV and hvv.
  • The tourist board's pages disagree with each other on its "11" Magic Cities (12 are listed), on Berlin's population and on whether Cologne passed a million. Details below.

What changes in Germany itself

These are the local facts that decide which app you open, each from an official tourism, rail or transit page.

Language: German, with English widely spoken

The germany.travel information page says the official language is German, that standard German is understood throughout the country, and that a large proportion of the population also speaks English, so language barriers "shouldn't pose a problem" for many visitors. The BVG, MVV, hvv and DB pages I read each had an English version, and the DB Navigator and Google Maps listings are in English. I did not open the apps, so I cannot say how much German shows up inside them.

Rail: one big operator, one national ticket

The tourist board's getting-around text describes a rail network of approximately 40,000 kilometres with around 5,700 stations. It names Deutsche Bahn as the main provider, with trains from other private and public operators on the same tracks, and calls bus travel another affordable option. That is the case for DB Navigator on a trip with several cities: its listing calls it a planner for Germany and beyond and says you can book local and long-distance tickets in it. I did not test whether it shows the other operators' trains.

City transit: one operator and one app per city

Inside a city you meet a different system each time. BVG runs Berlin and lists its tickets by name. MVV describes "1 network. 1 timetable. 1 fare." for Munich. hvv offers several apps for Hamburg, from the all-in-one hvv switch to apps for visually and hearing impaired passengers. The same tourist board text adds that many cities and regions give overnight guests a GuestCard with cheaper or no-charge regional transit tickets, and that the Deutschland-Ticket covers local transit across the country. Check your accommodation for a GuestCard before you buy anything.

Sights: a dozen big cities, each with its own kind of trip

The Magic Cities page profiles the large cities visitors pick for city breaks. Berlin is described by the Brandenburg Gate and an arts scene, Cologne by Carnival, Kölsch beer and the cathedral, Dresden by its cultural treasures, Leipzig by its musical heritage (Bach, Wagner, Mendelssohn), Nuremberg as a museum of history and Stuttgart by cars. The metropolises page adds the Reichstag dome and Museum Island in Berlin, the Elbphilharmonie, the Speicherstadt World Heritage Site and Miniatur Wunderland in Hamburg, and the Residenz, Nymphenburg Palace, the Deutsches Museum, beer gardens and the Oktoberfest in Munich. The sights are spread across cities that each need their own transit app, so what you want to see matters before you choose what to install.

Cycling: a lot of paths, and a ticket that excludes bikes

The same getting-around text says around 100,000 kilometres of bike paths and about 600 themed routes cross the country, and points to radroutenplaner-deutschland.de for planning tours. The European Consumer Centre says bicycles and dogs are not included in the Deutschland-Ticket and a bicycle day ticket is needed. Of the six apps here, only Google Maps lists cycling directions.

The Deutschland-Ticket: what the pages say

It is a ticket, not an app, but it decides what your apps have to do. These are the facts as read on 15 November 2026.

  • Price: €63 per month, and €66.80 per month from 1 January 2027, per DB. The European Consumer Centre Germany page, updated 13 April 2026, gives the same €63.
  • Where it works: all local public transport nationwide, not ICE or IC/EC, per DB. The Consumer Centre adds that it covers regional trains, buses, trams and underground trains, that it is valid in second class only, and that a few border stations are exceptions to its no-cross-border rule.
  • How you buy it: it is a subscription. The Consumer Centre says it is sold on the DB website, in DB Navigator or by regional and local operators, that it renews monthly unless you cancel by the 10th, that it is personal, and that payment is debited from your bank account. Whether a visitor without a German bank account can subscribe is not answered on the pages I read.
  • The 10th matters: the Consumer Centre warns that if you subscribe after the 10th, the cancellation date for that month has already passed, so you can be charged for two months even if you need one.

One wording problem: the tourist board's getting-around text says the Deutschland-Ticket "provides free travel" on local transit. Next to the price on DB's page, I read that as unlimited rides for the monthly fee, not no charge.

Where the official pages disagree

  1. How many Magic Cities. The page's heading says 11, and the text lists 12: Berlin, Bremen, Cologne, Dresden, Düsseldorf, Frankfurt, Hamburg, Hanover, Leipzig, Munich, Nuremberg and Stuttgart.
  2. Berlin's population. The metropolises page says 3.6 million. The information page says 3.4 million.
  3. Cologne. The metropolises page says it has been among cities with over a million inhabitants since 2010. The information page says Cologne "falls slightly short" of a million.

None of this changes which app you need. It does mean I quote no population figures as fact.

The apps, one at a time

DB Navigator: trains and the national ticket

The Play listing names Deutsche Bahn as the developer and lists booking tickets for local and long-distance travel, subscribing to and managing the Deutschland-Ticket, real-time timetables and platform details, a Journey Companion that sends updates on a saved connection, coach sequence information, a demand indicator for many trains and Komfort Check-in for long-distance trips. It also lists renting e-scooters and bikes and says an integrated map shows walking routes to nearby stops and stations. It advertises Germany-wide travel "from as little as €6.99" without saying on which routes. The listing showed 4.5 from 367K reviews and 50M+ downloads, updated 6 November 2026. The text I read says nothing about offline use.

BVG Fahrinfo: Berlin

BVG's page says the app shows the fastest connection to your destination and lets you buy the ticket directly. You can save a start stop, set an alarm for changes on a planned route, see your vehicle's position in a live timetable display and use live navigation. The ticket list includes 24-hour tickets, single and short-trip tickets, a 4-trip ticket, a 24-hour small group ticket, WelcomeCards, CityTourCards, bicycle tickets and monthly tickets for tariff zone AB. To pay by SEPA or credit card you register with an e-mail address; Apple Pay, Google Pay, SEPA direct debit, PayPal and credit card are the listed payment options. BVG also has a purchase-only Ticket-App and a Jelbi app in its menu, which I did not open.

MVV app: Munich

MVV's page says the app combines timetables, real-time information, disruption alerts, network maps and mobile tickets across the whole MVV network, shows how full a bus will be, and books on-demand services such as FLEX and FLEXlinie. Tickets come from an integrated shop, including day tickets, multi-trip tickets and short-distance tickets, paid with Apple Pay, Google Pay or other methods. The page calls the app ad-free and available at no charge from the Apple App Store, Google Play and the Huawei App Gallery. Its "MVVswipe" is a swipe to pick the right ticket, not a way to choose places.

hvv switch: Hamburg

hvv's apps page describes hvv switch as tickets, route planning and more, including buying an hvv Deutschlandticket and finding on-demand offers, and a separate hvv App for tickets and connection information. It also lists apps for visually and hearing impaired passengers and several on-demand apps for outlying areas. I read the page in English and did not open the app, so I cannot say which language the app uses.

Google Maps: one familiar planner

The Play listing describes directions for driving, walking, cycling and public transport with real-time updates, bike or scooter rentals, custom lists of saved places, and offline maps for areas with bad signal, and adds that some features are not available in all countries or cities. Its help page says that offline, transit, bicycling and walking directions are unavailable and that some countries or regions cannot be downloaded. That matters in a country of many separate transit systems if your roaming data is off. I did not test transit in any German city, so I cannot say how it compares with BVG, MVV or hvv. Where NomadSwipe fits (and where it does not)

For choosing places, not for getting around

I built NomadSwipe, so weigh this with that in mind. It is an Android app, and it works in any city worldwide. Pick a city and it deals real places one at a time: swipe right or tap Save to keep one, and every saved place is pinned on a trip map where you build a trip. Each place shows category, rating, distance and, where hours are known, whether it is open, and the list sorts by rating, distance or A to Z. You can add a custom pin for anything it does not show, such as your hotel. If you know you want Berlin or Munich but not what to fill the days with, picking places is the job it does.

What it does not do matters more for this query. It sells no tickets, so it does nothing for the Deutschland-Ticket or a long-distance train. It does not plan rail or metro journeys and it will not tell you about a disruption. Its listing says you can open a place and start directions, but I did not test that, and it is not a replacement for DB Navigator or a city app. The listing says up to 20 saved trips are available offline after you open them online, and the copies expire; it describes no downloadable maps. iPhone is coming soon.

Who should pick which

  • You do not know what to see yet: NomadSwipe to pick places, next to a transit app. I make no claim that its saved list moves into them automatically.
  • You will move between German cities by train: DB Navigator, and read the Deutschland-Ticket limits first, since DB's page says it is not valid in ICE or IC/EC.
  • You will stay a month and ride local transit daily: price the Deutschland-Ticket against what your city's operator sells, using DB's €63 and the operator's own price page, which I did not read.
  • One week in Berlin: BVG Fahrinfo, which lists WelcomeCards and 24-hour tickets by name.
  • A few days in Munich: the MVV app, which lists day tickets and shows how full a bus will be.
  • A few days in Hamburg: hvv switch, per hvv's apps page.
  • You want one familiar app for walking, cycling and reviews: Google Maps, mindful of its offline limits.
  • You are cycling the country: none of the six is a cycling route planner; the tourist board points to radroutenplaner-deutschland.de.

The short version

  • No single app covers Germany: rail, city transit, the national ticket and choosing places are separate jobs.
  • The Deutschland-Ticket is €63 a month now and €66.80 from 1 January 2027, per DB, and it does not cover ICE or IC/EC trains.
  • Berlin, Munich and Hamburg each have their own operator app that sells tickets.
  • German is the official language and a large proportion speak English, per the tourist board.
  • NomadSwipe, which I built, is Android only and covers choosing places, not tickets or transit.

FAQ

What is the best travel app for Germany?

There is no single best one, because a trip needs different jobs done. For trains and the Deutschland-Ticket, DB Navigator. For buses, trams and metro inside a city, that city's own app: BVG Fahrinfo in Berlin, the MVV app in Munich or hvv switch in Hamburg. For choosing what to see, I built NomadSwipe (Android), which only does that last job. I installed none of these apps, so this is what their pages say.

Do I need DB Navigator in Germany?

Only if you will ride trains or want the Deutschland-Ticket. Its Google Play listing says you can book tickets for local and long-distance travel in the app, subscribe to the Deutschland-Ticket, and see real-time timetables, platform details and coach sequence. If you stay in one city and walk or use its own transit app, you may not need it.

Does the Deutschland-Ticket cover ICE and IC trains?

No. DB's Deutschland-Ticket page says it is valid on all local public transport but not in ICE or IC/EC trains, and the European Consumer Centre Germany adds that it is valid in second class only. Long journeys on it mean regional trains and several changes.

How much is the Deutschland-Ticket?

DB's page says €63 per month, rising to €66.80 per month from 1 January 2027. The European Consumer Centre Germany says it is sold only as a subscription that renews unless you cancel by the 10th of the month, and that it is personal. I did not check whether a visitor without a German bank account can subscribe, so confirm that on bahn.de before you plan around it.

Do I need to speak German to use travel apps in Germany?

The pages I read suggest not. The BVG, MVV, hvv and DB pages I read all had English versions, and germany.travel says standard German is understood throughout the country and that a large proportion of the population also speaks English. I did not open the apps, so I cannot say how much German appears inside them.

Which app do I use for the metro and buses in Berlin, Munich or Hamburg?

Each city has its own operator and app. BVG Fahrinfo covers Berlin, the MVV app covers Munich and hvv switch covers Hamburg, and each of the three pages says its app sells tickets. DB Navigator can also plan local connections and sells the Deutschland-Ticket, per its listing.

Does Google Maps work offline in Germany?

Partly, with limits that apply everywhere. Google's help page says that offline, transit, bicycling and walking directions are unavailable, that driving offline gives no traffic info or alternate routes, and that some countries or regions cannot be downloaded. It does not name Germany, and I did not test a download.
